The following is my initial *reading list*, these are the books I like as my personal *Great Books Curriculum*, based on the ideas described [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Books_of_the_Western_World).

My foundation consists of the following three books:

1. **Adler, _How to Read a Book_** → how to approach books
2. **Ahrens, _How to Take Smart Notes_** → how to turn reading into a knowledge system
3. **Doto, _A System for Writing_** → how to turn that knowledge into your own thinking and writing

After that, I would move increasingly from **method → philosophy → civilization → literature → science → synthesis**.

## The overall architecture

Overall structure:

|Stage|Function|Main question|
|---|---|---|
|0|Reading & knowledge system|How should I read and think?|
|1|Intellectual foundations|What is philosophy and how do philosophers reason?|
|2|Ethics & the good life|How should a human being live?|
|3|Knowledge & interpretation|What can we know, and how do we interpret things?|
|4|Politics & society|How should society be organized?|
|5|History & civilization|How did the world we inhabit develop?|
|6|Human nature|What are humans like?|
|7|Literature|What can fiction reveal that arguments cannot?|
|8|Science & modernity|How do we understand the natural world?|
|9|Art, creativity & information|How do humans create and organize meaning?|
|10|Synthesis|How do all these disciplines fit together?|

The important thing is that **you don't need to finish one category before touching another**. The order should create intellectual dependencies.

# A. Laying the foundation

### 1. Mortimer Adler & Charles Van Doren, _How to Read a Book_

The operating manual for the entire project:

- [[inspectional reading]] 5500/4a
- [[analytical reading]] 
- [[syntopical reading]]
- [[the four questions]] 5500/4b
- identifying an author's problem
- propositions and arguments
- coming to terms with an author
- finding disagreements **between authors**

The most important idea for the eventual project is **syntopical reading**. My ultimate goal isn't merely to read 100 "great books", but to have multiple books answering the same questions.

### 2. Sönke Ahrens, _How to Take Smart Notes_

This comes immediately after Adler. The conceptual progression is:

**Adler:** How do I read? → followed by **Ahrens:** How do I retain and connect what I read?

### 3. Bob Doto, _A System for Writing_

Then: **How do I turn those connected ideas into my own arguments?**

Now we have:

> Reading → notes → thinking/writing

These three books are the foundation for the project.

# B. Greece: Socrates, Plato and the Stoics

### 4. Plato, _Apology_

Short, but foundational:

- Socrates
- philosophical inquiry
- intellectual humility
- examination of one's life
- argument versus reputation
- the philosopher and society

### 5. Plato, _Republic_

One of my**central books**, it connects:

- ethics
- psychology
- education
- politics
- epistemology
- metaphysics
- literature
- the nature of justice

### 6. Epictetus, _Enchiridion_

We move from:

> "What is philosophy?"

to:

> "How should I actually live?"

### 7. Seneca, _Letters from a Stoic_

Much richer than the _Enchiridion_, it connects philosophy with everyday life.

### 8. Marcus Aurelius, _Meditations_

I have read this before, but it is a foundational text, and reading it right after Seneca and Epictetus creates a conceptual framework for understanding what Marcus is doing.

### 9. Cicero, _How to Be a Friend / De Amicitia_

One of my all time favourites (my first classical fav)

We move from Stoic/Greco-Roman philosophy toward **human relationships and social life**.

# 3. The problem of knowledge

Now we introduce the question:

> **How do we know that what we think we know is actually true?**

### 10. Sextus Empiricus, _Outlines of Pyrrhonism_

Haven't yet read, but the plan is to take skepticism seriously.

Then:

### 11. Montaigne, _Essays_

Montaigne should be much more interesting after Plato, Stoicism and skepticism.

He is almost a laboratory for:

> How does a person think about himself and the world when certainty is difficult?

And he is the father of the essay, how cool is that?

### 12. Ralph Waldo Emerson, _Essays_

Another major conception of the independent thinker.

### 13. Michael Polanyi, _Personal Knowledge_

Now you're prepared for a much more sophisticated question:

> **Is objective knowledge actually possible without the participation of the knower?**

This creates a bridge into modern philosophy of science and [[epistemology]].

# 4. Ethics, meaning and the human condition

At this point I to my modern books:

### 14. Viktor Frankl, _Man's Search for Meaning_

### 15. Donald Robertson, _How to Think Like a Roman Emperor_

Loved this one, from here I found [[Ryan Holiday]]

### 16. Donald Robertson, _How to Think Like Socrates_

### 17. Ryan Holiday, _The Obstacle Is the Way_

### 18. Ryan Holiday, _Ego Is the Enemy_

### 19. Ryan Holiday, _Stillness Is the Key_

The three Holiday books are not three major philosophical milestones. They're modern interpretations of ideas we've already encountered in the primary sources. These books are an excellent example how the great books carry over in modern-day life.

This distinction is important for a Great Books framework:

> **Primary source → interpretation → modern application**

rather than treating all three as equivalent. They all have there place.

# 6. History and civilization

This is where I introduce the biggest historical works.

### 22. Gibbon, _The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire_

An old favourite, read it in Autumn the first time, the perfect series to read when the weather is terrible, lying in bed, just like Gibbon.

I'll read the shortened version. Gibbon is fun to read, but he did like to hear himself speak.

Then:

### 23. Richard Tarnas, _The Passion of the Western Mind_

This is particularly useful for my project because it gives a historical map of Western intellectual development.

We'll treat it as a **secondary guides** to accompany this Great Books project.

Your sequence becomes:

> **Greece → Rome → Christianity → Renaissance → Enlightenment → Romanticism → modernity**

That makes many later authors considerably easier to place.
